C

Where the unit goes

The camera in the lid needs open sky, so the whole unit goes where the sky is open. The rule is angle, not height: anything that rises Δh above the lens at distance d blocks up to atan(Δh/d). Buildings already take the bottom 10–15° of every Kathmandu horizon, so anything kept under 15° is free. For that, d ≥ 3.7 × Δh.

First choice: a parapet or the stairwell roof, far enough from the tank. Strap a shelf to it, and the unit needs no bracket at all. It also puts the unit near the stair door, so maintenance is easy.

Fallback: beside the tank, on a short GI pipe clamped to the stand corner, with the lens above the tank top. Use it only if no open spot exists. It's more metal and more height, and the box is fully exposed up there.

D

The 110 mm ribbon: careful placement

The board covers the camera connector. On a Pi 3B+, the connector sits about 44 mm from the power end. Your board stops 23 mm short of the USB/Ethernet end (BOARD-BUILD), so the connector is ~20 mm under the board. The ribbon has to leave the connector, run under the board, and come out at the board's A-edge. That edge is ~4 mm in front of the USB jack, the only gap on that side.

Only one orientation can reach. The Pi goes on the inside wall with the USB/Ethernet end up, close under the lid. The ribbon comes out at the A-edge, climbs past the USB jack faces, and goes through the lid into a short tower. The estimated route is ~100–110 mm, which uses all of a 110 mm ribbon. Hanging the Pi from the lid (sheet 4) does not work with this board: the ribbon would have to go round the jacks and back.

Correction: the HANDOFF and roofbox_check.py "~90 mm, reaches" result was computed for a 65 × 70 board that didn't cover the connector. It doesn't apply to the 100 × 150 board you are building.

Paper-strip test, before anything is fixed

  • Cut a 110 mm paper strip, 16 mm wide. Plug the board onto the Pi.
  • Tuck one end where the ribbon enters the Pi's camera connector. Fold it flat under the board, out at the A-edge, and up past the USB jacks.
  • Hold the Pi + board against the inside wall, USB end up, as close under the lid as the jacks allow. Mark where the strip's end meets the lid. That's where the tower bore goes.
  • Allow ~4 mm at each end for the connectors, and ~15 mm from the lid face to the camera board inside a short tower.
  • Pass: the strip reaches with ≥5 mm to spare. Fail: the 30 cm ribbon (Himalayan Solution, in stock) is the cheap fix. Nothing else gets you round the board.

Opening the box: with a 110 mm ribbon the lid can't lift far, so unplug the ribbon at the camera end first. That connector has a latch; handle it gently. It's fine for occasional service, not for weekly opening.

E

Power, as built

Decided: no new supply. The 5 V 2 A barrel adapter feeds the Pi through the board's GPIO feed ((47,W) → pins 2/4), with the 1000 µF at the feed and 3.3 V from the Pi on rail 43. The MQ heater rail (41) takes the other 5 V source, the USB charger with its lead cut short, grounds joined at rail 45. That's BOARD-BUILD §2 as it stands.

On 13 Sep this was clean at idle and undervolted under a sustained 4-core load. The camera pipeline is a sustained load, so make it a lighter one. All of the following is free:

  • arm_freq=1000 in config.txt, down from 1400 MHz. Less current at peak.
  • Bluetooth off (dtoverlay=disable-bt). No HDMI display attached in the field.
  • The sky pipeline runs at 640 × 480 and a few frames per second, on one core.
  • Shortest 5 V lead possible: put the adapter in its own small box right beside the unit, and run the mains extension to it. Extend the mains side, never the 5 V side.
  • Log get_throttled every minute as a health channel, and flag readings and sky events recorded during undervoltage. The data stays honest even when power isn't perfect.

Gate, and what happens if it fails

With the caps applied and the pipeline running, vcgencmd get_throttled reads 0x0 for an hour.

  • Fails: swap the two 5 V sources, so the USB charger feeds the Pi and the barrel adapter feeds the heaters. Retest.
  • Still fails: run the sky pipeline only by day and in short night windows. Weather logging is light and should pass on its own.
  • Last resort: an LM2596 buck converter with the 12 V adapter, about ₨150–200 at SLT. Note it, don't buy it now.

Dew heater on the heater rail

Eight 220 Ω ¼ W resistors in parallel on rail 41 (5 V): ≈0.9 W total, 0.11 W each, 0.18 A from the heater supply, nothing from the Pi's. Bed them around the lens ring. Bill 724's 30 unlabelled resistors probably include 220–330 Ω, the usual LED values, so meter them first. 330 Ω gives ≈0.6 W.

F

Fisheye, tower and hood

✓ The camera and fisheye both work (5 Oct), so F6 is closed. The OV5647 board is 25 × 24 mm, and the fisheye's knurled ring sits on a flat clip arm.

  • If the ring unscrews from the arm: seat it under a 2 mm lip at the tower top, bedded in neutral-cure silicone.
  • If it doesn't: the flat arm is the mounting plate. Bolt or zip-tie it across the tower top with the lens centred over the camera, and seal under it.
  • Set the gap between the fisheye and the camera's lens (≈3 mm, U3) with washers.
  • Short tower: ≈20 mm of 50 mm PVC offcut through the lid, with a drilled end cap. Short is not a style choice: every millimetre of tower is ribbon you don't have.
  • Low hood: a corrugated-plastic or tin offcut ≈290 mm square, on four sticks or bolts from the shelf, a few mm above the lid. It has a hole for the tower, so the lens sits just above it. It shades the box and keeps rain off the gasket without standing in the lens's view.
  • Grab one fisheye frame and note how much of the sensor the image circle fills. That's the field of view to plan the spikes and hood edge around.

J

Everything that crosses the sky

One service, hics-sky, separate from weather logging, builds on sensors/camera.py (capture_sky(), stream_frames()):

  1. CaptureNight video, 2×2-binned 1296 × 972, ~5 fps, 0.2 s exposures, fixed gain, a ~10 s ring buffer. By day, a still every few minutes for cloud cover and sunshine.
  2. CompressPer 256-frame block, keep max-pixel, frame-of-max, mean and standard deviation (the Global Meteor Network's idea).
  3. Detect + trackThreshold, find lines and blobs, link them into tracks.
  4. IdentifyPlanets, Moon and stars by ephemeris. Satellites from CelesTrak orbit data. Aircraft by slow, blinking tracks. Meteors as fast, straight and not blinking. Lightning as a whole-frame flash. Lens dirt as a patch that stays fixed while the sky turns.
  5. CalibrateSun track by day, refined nightly from planets, stars and satellite passes.
  6. LogAn events table: time, class, confidence, alt-az track, brightness, and a clip for meteors and unknowns. Meteors stay candidates (G9).
